Navigating American Culture as an English Outsider

A reflection on belonging, observation, and the cultural codes that shape identity in a foreign place.

Essay · Sukira Harris · 9 min read

Moving to the US for university felt like it would be a straightforward transition. After all, I’d attended an international school my whole life, where I was the only English person in my year. I was already familiar with American culture as nearly 40% of my classmates were American. I figured we spoke the same language, so how different could things really be? I couldn’t have been more mistaken. Almost immediately, I realized I had to translate my world into terms that fit the American mindset, noticing things I had previously overlooked. At my international school, everyone adjusted to UK norms without question, and I never fully understood the challenges that international students must have faced as they adapted.

This process is referred to as transculturation where the dominant culture is adopted and modified by subordinated cultures. Although to me, this phenomenon had always seemed invisible. Mary Louise Pratt outlined the experience of transculturation in her piece, “Arts of the Contact Zone.” In this, Pratt depicts how each culture has its own specific zone and when these cultures meet it can be referred to as ‘contact zones’.

Pratt outlines how when different cultures meet they often influence one another, which often leads to conflict. The influence between cultures is rarely even, creating a power imbalance between the dominant culture as it imposes its norms onto subordinated cultures. This imposition of cultural values may lead to a culture having to reshape their own identities, which is often never passive. Adaptation is often accompanied by resistance and outright conflict while cultures attempt to maintain their unique identities. Pratt also describes each culture containing ‘safe houses’ which are environments in which individuals can maintain their own cultural identity without outside influence. These safe houses are a space where the subordinated cultures can reclaim their identities, providing cultural autonomy amid the pressure of the contact zones.

Ironically, being on the dominant side of transculturation in my international school I hadn't even realised it was happening. Moving to the US made me realize that a shared language doesn’t always equal a shared perspective. The simple things caught me off guard, like measurements; but there were also unexpected adjustments such as fashion, customer service, and overall politeness.

Fashion, for instance, was a major culture shock. Back home, I would never see people wearing “joggers” (what Americans call “sweatpants”) outside their house. It might seem minor, but it was baffling to see people casually stepping out in what I’d consider pajamas. In London, it’s rare to see anyone walking around in joggers, especially not to school or just for everyday errands.

It’s not just that I was surprised by their fashion, but I felt my style also surprised others. After my second week of college, a friend I had made from New York said to me, “It’s so funny that you wear pants all the time. I mean I don’t even own a pair.” Puzzled, I replied with, “What do you mean?” She responded, “Well, I’d only wear them if I was going to an extremely formal event.” It had not dawned on me that this was not the social norm here. In London, everyone dresses up every day in a casual but formal look. That was the first time I found myself questioning how I dressed. I love fashion and always have but it was weird to hear that how I was dressing was I suppose over the top and maybe too much.

I’ve even had people ask me where I was going after class, but I wasn’t going anywhere, that's just how I dressed in day to day life. Once I even had someone stop me on the street and told me that I looked like a lawyer in just a normal casual outfit. It feels strange and slightly uncomfortable to know that my style doesn't exactly fit in. But then I remind myself that I enjoy how I dress and I don’t want to change it to satisfy other people's expectations or social norms in fashion.

Linking back to Pratt’s concepts of power dynamics, as the subordinate culture I felt pressure to start dressing more casually. Something as subtle as what we wear is an example of how power dynamics manifest in contact zones. Even though it was never explicitly stated to me to change how I dress, through everyday interactions between my peers and friends carried an implicit message that my style didn't fit the norm. The unspoken expectation fostered an internal conflict between maintaining my culture and blending in with the dominant American culture. The experience of being ‘lawyer-like’ underscored the cultural dynamic by making me aware of the set expectations leading me to feeling out of place.

This was only the beginning. My discomfort deepened when I encountered the American approach to customer service, which I found to be quite unsettling. In the U.S., customer service workers aim to be friendly and talkative although for me it felt very intrusive. These are essential parts of the social experience, as workers only make money off tips so it is essential that they be as friendly as possible. I felt this new pressure curated an atmosphere where interactions were purely transactional. In return, I felt as if I had to match this level of friendliness which oftentimes was exhausting. In contrast, traditional UK values emphasize personal space as a form of respect. Here, customer service becomes a contact zone in itself, a place where my own cultural norms collide with the dominant American ones. Unlike other areas of transculturation, there was no escape from this one. I had to just consistently confront this new form of customer service until I eventually accepted it. There was no “safe house” to escape to, instead I had to adapt and leave my own standards behind.

I vividly remember arriving in the US a week before classes with my Dad to get familiar with my surroundings, and spend some time exploring Boston. On our first day out we went to a restaurant and the waiter came over and asked, “How are you guys doing today?” My Dad and I were both startled and confused and did not really know what to do. Were we supposed to respond and ask the waiter back how they are? We simply asked to order, unaware that the polite thing to do was to engage in conversation, leading to some awkwardness. This seemingly small encounter highlighted how I was stepping into a contact zone where the American norms dominated.

Since it was my first encounter I didn’t think too much about it. Then it was everywhere, even at coffee shops and in stores with shopping assistants. A moment that particularly stood out for me was when a shopping assistant came up to me and offered to hold my clothes in a changing room for me. Initially I was puzzled with the social etiquette, was she trying to befriend me, or was this just part of her job? I immediately felt that my personal space was invaded without warning. I felt this new subtle pressure to run to the changing room, to match the assistant’s eagerness. At that moment, what Americans saw as friendly felt to me like an overstep of personal boundaries. This difference in perception between customer service highlights the meeting of contact zones that Pratt outlined. In these moments, I had no option but to conform to the American social norms, rejecting my own culture's practices.

For me, shopping is done quietly and privately with the freedom of browsing without the need to interact. It allows a private space where I can turn off and just relax. Although, I now have to be aware this is no longer going to be my experience. I had to adjust my behavior to align with their friendly standards, feeling the subtle pressure that was placed on me. The power dynamic between the two cultures in the contact zone is clear, leaving the subordinated culture feeling out of sync.

This same dynamic extends to everyday interactions. I found Americans much more inclined to give compliments to strangers, which is a rarity back in the UK. I found myself receiving a lot more compliments in the U.S. than usual, such as, “I love that bag” or “OMG, Where did you get that sweater?” It made me realise that compliments in the U.S. are a way to initiate conversations and to build connections. Compliments are usually only given to close friends, family members or special occasions in the UK. Additionally, compliments being given out so frequently made them feel less genuine and more routine.

I often found myself questioning if I was being unfriendly or aloof in daily social interactions, especially in class projects or labs where collaboration is essential. I was in a physics lab on the first week, and my partner did something wrong so I told them, “That’s incorrect, you should fix that.” They responded with, “Damn, okay,” which left me confused. They proceeded to tell me that I was quite blunt, and made me acknowledge that direct communication differed here. People often would couple feedback with positive reinforcement, I would later find. It was strange that what I would consider as straight-forward and effective others would consider as harsh. It was the first time I had to make a conscious effort to be more gentle with my words while also being socially open and friendly to strangers.

In this case, the contact zone became my politeness and communication style impacted by my cultural background. In the lab, what my background would value as effectiveness in a formal setting would manifest as rude in the more dominant American norm. The power imbalance led to me as the subordinate culture adapting my language and softening it to be more friendly. This interaction further highlights how everyday interactions subtly pressure the subordinate culture to alter their behaviours and mannerisms.

This contrast emphasises how the dominant culture shapes the interactions in the contact zones that Pratt outlined. What Americans view as friendly, English people will often perceive to be quite intrusive. Conversely, what is perceived as respectful in English culture may appear as distant and rude in American culture. This dynamic further accentuates the power within contact zones, enforcing the subordinated cultures to adapt and reshape their own norms and identities in the process.

Moving to the US for university has been an eye-opening experience highlighting the transculturation my peers at my international school were experiencing daily. Each contact zone I navigated whether that be fashion, customer service or everyday politeness, acted as a constant reminder that I must conform to the new values being placed on me. These moments underscored Pratt’s concept of contact zones, which results in power imbalances with an imposition of power by the dominant culture.

The experience of adapting a new culture extends beyond surface level adjustments, and permeates into deep core values and behaviors. The constant pressure from the dominant culture, imposing its own values, often takes precedence over one's identity and beliefs, leading to a re-evaluation. As Pratt discusses, “the power dynamics within these contact zones often compel subordinated cultures to reshape themselves to fit in,” sometimes at the cost of their own cultural autonomy (Pratt 35).

Reflecting on my own journey, I have attempted to maintain a balance by adapting to the new cultural norms while retaining parts of my identity. I have learnt to adjust how I interact with what is deemed socially acceptable, but I strive to still hold on to parts of my identity that make me who I am. Understanding that both adaptation is a necessity but the rejection of one’s own culture is not, it is important to allow self-expression of my own culture. For example, I consciously maintain my sense of fashion, as it is an integral part of me even if it doesn't align with U.S. societal norms. These realizations have enriched my experiences where multiple cultures intersect, allowing me to be more aware and acknowledge the power imbalances in these contact zones.
