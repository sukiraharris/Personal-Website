Radical vs. Transformative Consumption

The Spiritual Myth of the American Adam in Nike and Lululemon.

Essay · Sukira Harris · 6 min read

This article examines how Nike's radical consumption model, focused on societal change, contrasts with Lululemon's transformative consumption, centered on personal growth. By analysing these branding strategies, I will assess how they both support or challenge Roy Suddaby's concept of the spiritual myth of consumption in modern culture (Suddaby 410). Drawing on the American Adam cultural archetype, both brands offer distinct avenues to self-reinvention. Nike has a stronger outward focus on global change in contrast to Lululemon's introspective journey of moral and personal development. Nike’s radical consumption model encourages consumers to enhance themselves while contributing to the greater good, specifically through taking a stand for social issues like justice and equality. In contrast, Lululemon's transformative consumption promotes an inward approach using self-reflection through yoga and mindfulness to achieve inner harmony. Through campaigns like Nike’s “Just Do It” and Lululemon’s “This is Yoga,” both brands tap into the transformative power of consumption, though they appeal to different aspects of the consumer’s desire for self-transformation.

The American Adam cultural archetype prioritises self-reinvention alongside moral perfection, symbolising the idea that individuals can remake themselves in society from a fresh start. Through consumption, individuals strive for greatness in the image of the American Adam, ultimately shaping society in the process. Nike’s outward focus on global change closely aligns with American Adam’s pursuit of social transformation. On the other hand, Lululemon's focus is rooted in the introspective side of the myth, emphasising moral perfection through self-reliance and personal growth. Both brands offer distinct paths to self-transformation; Nike aims to have a broader societal impact, whereas Lululemon focuses on individual self-improvement.

The spiritual myth defined by Suddaby suggests that goods not only fulfil material needs but also transcend into spiritual fulfilment. This concept implies that material goods can lead to a sense of moral satisfaction and personal growth. Suddaby describes transformational consumers as “a massive and growing group of people who see life as a never-ending series of projects to live healthier, wealthier, wiser lives (Suddaby 417).” Suddaby highlights that the consumers are enticed to the spiritual myth because it offers opportunities for personal improvement. Both Nike and Lululemon tap into this myth by selling products that act as tools for individuals to utilize for self-improvement and the reshaping of identity. Both brands heavily rely on the consumer’s desire to transform oneself, promoting the idea that purchases can lead to a better version of oneself.

Nike is an intergenerational athletic brand that leverages radical consumption to drive social change, encouraging its consumers to strive for greatness. To achieve this, Nike incorporates the spiritual myth of consumption, as detailed by Suddaby, into its advertising. As Suddaby explains, “Radical consumption is built on the premise that consumption is not just a private act of self-gratification but a public act of moral expression (Suddaby 415).” By extending beyond being a sportswear company, Nike encourages its consumers to view their purchases as a way to engage with larger societal issues. Nike’s advertising appeals to both the self-driven, empowered individual and those seeking the chance to influence the world. This is clearly illustrated in Nike's “Dream Crazy” campaign with Colin Kaepernick, a former NFL quarterback. The campaign's slogan is to, “Believe in something, even if it means sacrificing everything,” reflecting Kaepernick's decision to kneel during the national anthem, as a protest of racial injustice (Media Shower). By doing so, he utilised his platform to challenge the oppression that black people and people of colour face in America. Nike incorporated Kaepernick to align itself with the broader issue of racial inequality and to place its consumers in a position to make a difference through consumption. This example amplifies Nike’s radical consumption branding, allowing their consumers to feel as if they’re contributing to meaningful social activism through their involvement with the brand.

Not only is Nike’s radical consumption utilized in their advertising, but it can be directly seen in the company's slogan, “Just Do It.” This slogan serves as a call to action, urging consumers to challenge societal norms and step up for change. The slogan aligns with Nike's overall strategy of radical empowerment, positioning consumers as part of a larger societal movement. Nike's narrative is clear: when buying from their company, an individual is not just enhancing their physical abilities but also playing a transformative role in society. This message intertwines moral and social causes, reflected in Roy Suddaby's concept of radical consumption, where Nike places its customers in a position to make an impact beyond themselves.

Similarly, Lululemon, a clothing brand that is also rooted in the spiritual myth of the American Adam, focuses on wellness, mindfulness and inner balance. More than just a brand, Lululemon provides an inspiring platform for transformational consumers, promoting a holistic lifestyle integrating both mental and physical health. Suddaby highlighted the company's emphasis on mental health, detailing that Lululemon requires staff to re-examine personal goals twice a year, alongside a three-day personal transformation workshop. This focus on personal growth underscores Suddaby's definition of the spiritual myth: “material goods not only satisfy physical needs but also offer spiritual fulfilment, moral satisfaction, and a pathway to personal growth.”

These ideologies can be seen in the “This is Yoga” campaign, in which images are accompanied with words such as ‘self-discipline’ linking the brand to a broader spiritual narrative. Lululemon's executive vice president, Tom Walter, explained to Racked, “The intention is to show how yoga can take place off the mat and to celebrate the unexpected ways the practice empowers people around the globe to live a life of purpose” (Vasan). This further emphasises Lululemon's holistic approach extending beyond their product and integrating consumption with personal and spiritual development.

Furthermore, the brand's ambition to curate an environment of deep self-growth, creates a unique, almost cult-like experience, where consumption and self-improvement are interwoven. As Marcom Central notes, Lululemon markets itself as not just a brand but a “lifestyle,” with a mission to, “Elevate human potential by helping people feel their best,” emphasising Lululemon's commitment to well-being (Marcom). By promoting a holistic lifestyle centered on well-being, Lululemon aligns with Suddaby’s spiritual myth, offering transformational consumers a path to personal fulfillment.

Additionally, both brands have made significant efforts to target environmentally conscious consumers, integrating sustainability into their unique consumption models. Both Nike's refurbished footwear program and Lululemon's “Like New” program allow customers to feel as if they are making ethically responsible choices, reinforcing the idea that their purchases contribute to a greater good, in line with the spiritual myth of consumption (Lululemon).

More specifically, Nike utilises the concept of radical consumption by stating that contributions are, “Helping to protect the future of sport” and that “We all share the responsibility for our playground—Planet Earth” (Nike). Nike emphasises that by participating in environmental programs that consumers' individual actions, no matter how small, will continue to have a meaningful impact on the world.

Conversely, Lululemon maintains its introspective angle, consistent with its transformative consumption approach. Through the “Like New” program, Lululemon encourages the consumer to make environmentally responsible decisions while actively promoting personal growth. The brand states, “Start with gently used pieces you have to feel to believe, and just add layers.” This message emphasises Lululemon's commitment to a holistic lifestyle by tying consumption to personal growth and wellbeing. Lululemon offers the consumers a chance to feel good about the ethical decisions they make, turning each purchase into a broader journey toward self-optimisation and mindful living.

In conclusion, Nike and Lululemon offer two distinct yet powerful interpretations of the spiritual myth of consumption outlined by Roy Suddaby, offering consumers pathways extending beyond products. Nike's radical consumption drives societal change, positioning its consumers as advocates for justice and equality. However, Lululemon's transformative approach focuses on fostering growth and inner balance. Both companies transform goods into tools for self-reinvention, whether it be on a societal or individual level, through the spiritual myth of consumption. The brands' aforementioned campaigns demonstrate that consumption can align personal values with larger causes, making purchasing decisions a form of moral and social expression. Furthermore, they both integrated sustainability into their models, signalling a shift toward ethical responsibility becoming a key facet of modern-day consumption. Ultimately, despite what consumers seek to change, whether it be themselves or the world, Nike and Lululemon offer a chance of transformation for consumers extending beyond material goods, revealing the evolving role of consumption in modern culture.
